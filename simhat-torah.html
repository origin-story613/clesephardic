What Is Simhat Torah? Understanding the Joy of Torah

The Meaning Behind Simhat Torah

Simhat Torah means “joy of the Torah” in Hebrew. It's the day when Jewish communities finish reading the entire Torah and immediately start again from the beginning. Think of it as both a graduation and a new beginning rolled into one celebration.

The highlight? Dancing with Torah scrolls in joyful processions called hakafot. Everyone gets a chance to carry or touch the Torah as the community circles the synagogue seven times. It's loud, festive, and full of singing.

How Simhat Torah Connects to Sukkot and Shemini Atzeret

These holidays flow together like chapters in a book:

  • Sukkot (7 days): You eat meals in the sukkah and celebrate the harvest
  • Shemini Atzeret (8th day): A separate holiday focused on prayer, including prayers for rain
  • Simhat Torah: Celebrates completing the Torah reading cycle (combined with Shemini Atzeret outside Israel)

When Is Simhat Torah in 2025 and Beyond?

Simhat Torah always falls in late September or October. In 2025, it begins the evening of October 13th. Outside Israel, it's observed on the second day of Shemini Atzeret.

Simhat Torah caps off nearly a month of fall holidays. It sends you back into regular life with the Torah's wisdom fresh in your heart.

Sephardic and Mizrachi Traditions for Simhat Torah

How Sephardic Communities Celebrate Differently

Sephardic Jews bring their own flavor to Simhat Torah. While the core celebration stays the same—dancing with the Torah—the style feels different. Sephardic communities often incorporate Middle Eastern and Mediterranean influences into their music and customs.

One major difference? The melodies. Ashkenazi communities might sing familiar Eastern European tunes, but Sephardic synagogues fill with songs in Ladino, Arabic, or Hebrew with distinct Middle Eastern scales. The vocal harmonies and rhythms reflect centuries of Jewish life across the Mediterranean and Middle East.

Music, Dancing, and Customs in Mizrachi Traditions

Mizrachi Jews from places like Iraq, Yemen, Syria, and Morocco each brought unique customs to Simhat Torah:

  • Music style: Expect upbeat Middle Eastern melodies with complex rhythms—all sung a cappella
  • Dance formations: Circle dances (like the hora) mixed with spontaneous individual dancing
  • Special poems: Piyyutim (liturgical poems) sung in between hakafot rounds
  • Children's role: Kids often lead processions with flags and toy Torahs. At Cleveland Sephardic Community, we teach children a couple of pasukim (verses) so they can read from the Torah and have their own aliyah—a meaningful milestone
  • Food traditions: Sweet treats symbolizing the sweetness of Torah study

The Hatan Torah and Hatan Bereshit Honors

Two community members receive special honors called “aliyot.” The Hatan Torah (groom of the Torah) reads the final verses of Deuteronomy. The Hatan Bereshit (groom of Genesis) reads the opening of Genesis. It's like passing the baton from ending to beginning.

These honorees are often celebrated by the entire community with special blessings and songs during the service.

Sephardic and Mizrachi traditions add layers of cultural richness that connect ancient Torah wisdom to centuries of diaspora experience.

The Three-Day Journey: Shemini Atzeret, Simhat Torah, and Isru Hag

Shemini Atzeret: The Eighth Day of Assembly

Shemini Atzeret comes right after the seven days of Sukkot. The name means “eighth day of assembly,” and it's actually a separate holiday with its own identity. This is when we say goodbye to the sukkah and transition indoors.

The day focuses on prayer, especially the prayer for rain (Geshem). In Israel, the rainy season begins around this time, so we ask for blessing and abundance. The liturgy shifts from the joyful harvest themes of Sukkot to prayers for sustenance in the coming year.

Simhat Torah: Celebrating the Torah

Outside of Israel, Simhat Torah is celebrated on the second day of Shemini Atzeret. In Israel, both holidays happen on the same day. This is when the energy shifts from solemn prayer to joyful celebration.

The Torah reading cycle completes and immediately begins again:

  • Final verses of Deuteronomy are read
  • Opening verses of Genesis follow right after
  • Seven hakafot (processions) circle the synagogue
  • Everyone dances, sings, and celebrates

Children wave flags. Adults take turns carrying Torah scrolls. The atmosphere is pure joy.

Isru Hag: Extending the Holiday Joy

Isru Hag literally means “bind the festival.” It's the day after Simhat Torah when we ease back into regular life. While not a full holiday, it carries a semi-festive mood.

Many communities avoid scheduling sad occasions like funerals on Isru Hag. Some people eat a slightly nicer meal than usual. Think of it as a gentle landing after weeks of holiday intensity.

These three days create a perfect ending to the fall holiday season—prayer, celebration, and a graceful return to everyday life.

The Sukkah to Simhat Torah Transition: Completing the Fall Holiday Cycle

From Dwelling in the Sukkah to Dancing with the Torah

For seven days during Sukkot, you eat meals in the sukkah—a temporary dwelling that reminds us of the fragile shelters our ancestors used in the desert. You look up through the roof covering and see the sky. You feel the breeze. It's intimate and vulnerable.

Then comes Simhat Torah, and suddenly you're dancing indoors with the entire community. The mood shifts from quiet reflection to explosive joy. You've gone from sitting under the stars to circling the synagogue with Torah scrolls held high.

Spiritual Themes That Connect the Holidays

Both holidays celebrate dependence and gratitude:

  • Sukkot: We depend on God for shelter, just as our ancestors did
  • Simhat Torah: We depend on God for wisdom through Torah

The sukkah represents physical protection. The Torah represents spiritual guidance. Together, they remind us that we need both to thrive.

The journey from Sukkot to Simhat Torah takes you from the vulnerability of the sukkah to the strength found in Torah study. It's a beautiful progression from body to soul, from shelter to wisdom.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Simhat Torah and Shemini Atzeret?

Shemini Atzeret is the eighth day after Sukkot, focusing on prayers for rain and marking the end of the harvest season. Simhat Torah celebrates completing the annual Torah reading cycle. Outside Israel, Simhat Torah is observed on the second day of Shemini Atzeret. In Israel, both are combined into one day. Think of Shemini Atzeret as solemn and prayerful, while Simhat Torah is joyful and celebratory.

When does Simhat Torah start and end in Cleveland?

Simhat Torah in Cleveland follows the diaspora calendar, beginning at sundown and ending at nightfall the following day. In 2025, it starts the evening of October 13th and ends the evening of October 14th. Service times vary by congregation, so contact Cleveland Sephardic Community directly for specific start times. The celebration typically includes evening and morning services with hakafot processions.

Do you need to be Sephardic to attend Simhat Torah at Cleveland Sephardic Community?

No, absolutely not. Cleveland Sephardic Community welcomes everyone regardless of background—Sephardic, Ashkenazi, or simply curious about Jewish traditions. You don't need any affiliation or membership to attend. First-time visitors are warmly welcomed and encouraged to participate in the dancing and celebrations. The community is happy to explain customs and help newcomers feel comfortable throughout the service.

What happens during the hakafot (Torah processions)?

Hakafot are seven joyful processions where community members carry Torah scrolls around the synagogue while everyone sings and dances. Each circuit (hakafah) features different songs and melodies. Adults take turns holding the scrolls while others dance alongside. Children wave flags and join the procession. Everyone touches or kisses the Torah as it passes. It's energetic, loud, and filled with celebration—the highlight of Simhat Torah.

Is Simhat Torah the same day in Israel and Cleveland?

No, the dates differ. In Israel, Simhat Torah coincides with Shemini Atzeret on a single day. In Cleveland and throughout the diaspora, Shemini Atzeret is observed for two days, with Simhat Torah falling on the second day. This means Cleveland celebrates Simhat Torah one day later than Israel. The difference stems from ancient uncertainty about the calendar outside Jerusalem.

What should I bring to Simhat Torah services?

You don't need to bring anything special—just yourself and comfortable shoes for dancing. Dress in synagogue-appropriate attire that's modest and respectful. If you have children, they might enjoy bringing a small Israeli flag, though many communities provide these. No food, prayer books, or special items are necessary. Just come ready to celebrate and participate in the joy.

How long do Simhat Torah services last?

Simhat Torah services typically last 2-3 hours, though timing varies by community. The evening service is usually shorter, while the morning service can extend longer due to multiple Torah readings and seven hakafot processions. The dancing and celebration can continue beyond the formal service if the community is particularly energetic. Contact Cleveland Sephardic Community for their specific schedule and expected duration.

What is Isru Hag and should I take off work?

Isru Hag is the semi-festive day immediately following Simhat Torah. The name means “bind the festival” and represents a gentle transition back to regular life. It's not a full holiday, so most people work normally. However, the day retains a slightly festive character—some avoid fasting or sad occasions. Think of it as an extended afterglow of the holidays rather than a day requiring time off.
